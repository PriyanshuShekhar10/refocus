You know what you need to do. The task is clear, the deadline is real, and you even want to do it. And yet you find yourself reorganizing your desktop, refilling a water glass you just filled, or "quickly" checking one thing that becomes forty minutes. The work isn't the problem. Starting is.

This is one of the least talked-about truths about focused work: the effort isn't evenly distributed. Most of the resistance is bunched up at the very beginning, in the gap between deciding to work and actually being in it.

## The first five minutes carry all the weight

Once you're a few paragraphs into the writing, or a few lines into the code, the task tends to pull you along. Momentum is real. The trouble is that momentum can't help you until you've already started — and the start is exactly where your brain throws up every small objection it can find.

Part of this is that starting requires loading the whole task into your head at once: what it is, where you left off, what "done" looks like. That initial load feels heavy, so you flinch away from it. Every time you flinch, you pay the loading cost again from scratch, which is why a morning of "almost starting" is so exhausting despite producing nothing.

If you only fix one thing about your focus, make it the start. Shrink it until it's almost embarrassingly small. Don't sit down to "write the report" — sit down to open the document and write one bad sentence. The goal of the first five minutes isn't progress; it's just to be *in* the task so momentum can take over.

## Willpower is a bad starting engine

The common advice is to "just focus" or "be more disciplined," as if attention were a muscle you could clench. But willpower is a terrible way to begin, because it's most depleted exactly when you need it — early in a hard task, before any reward has arrived. Relying on it means every session starts with a fight you often lose.

Structure works better than willpower because it moves the decision out of the moment. A fixed start time, a specific first action, and a defined stopping point mean you don't have to negotiate with yourself each time. The choice was made earlier, by a calmer version of you.

## Borrow accountability from someone else

Here's the thing willpower can't easily replace: the quiet pressure of another person in the room. When someone else is present and working, starting stops being optional in the same way. You sit down because they're sitting down. This is the old magic of libraries, coffee shops, and study halls — you're not being watched, exactly, but you're *accompanied*, and that's enough to tip you into the task.

This is also why body doubling works so well, especially for people who find solo starts brutal. You don't need conversation or supervision — just the presence of someone else who's also there to focus. If your friends aren't free when you need to work, a tool like [Refocus](https://refocus.co.in) pairs you with someone for a timed co-working session: you each say what you're working on, then work side by side with a shared timer and a short check-in at the end. It's accountability without the meeting energy, which turns out to be exactly what the first five minutes need.

## Make stopping as clear as starting

Ironically, one reason starting feels so heavy is that the task feels endless. If there's no edge to the work, your brain treats it as an infinite obligation and reasonably tries to avoid it. A fixed session length fixes this. "I'll work until the timer ends" is a promise you can keep, and knowing there's a finish line makes the start feel survivable.

Try 25 minutes when the resistance is high and 50 when you're warmer. The number matters less than the fact that it's *defined*. A bounded block is easy to begin because it's easy to end.

## Lower the stakes of the start

Most of us make starting harder than it needs to be by attaching too much meaning to it. This session has to be productive. This has to be the day I finally focus. That pressure is itself a distraction. The way through is almost anticlimactic: reduce the first action to something you can't fail at, remove the nearby escape routes (put the phone in another room), and let a timer and, ideally, another person carry the structure so you're not leaning on willpower alone.

You don't need to want it more. You need to make the beginning smaller, the ending clearer, and the room a little less empty. Do that, and showing up stops being the hardest part — it becomes the only part you have to get right.
